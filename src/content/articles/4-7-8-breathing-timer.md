## What 4-7-8 breathing actually is

4-7-8 is one pattern: inhale quietly through the nose for four counts, hold for seven, exhale through the mouth for eight<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. The counting is the whole technique — which is why it pairs so well with a [breathing pacer](/breathing-pacer) you can feel instead of watch.

Andrew Weil, M.D., popularised the exercise as the "Relaxing Breath." The tip of the tongue rests against the ridge behind the upper front teeth and stays there; you exhale around the tongue, making a soft whoosh. One inhale, one hold, one exhale is a single breath cycle, repeated three more times for a total of four<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. Cleveland Clinic describes the same pattern, tracing its roots to yogic pranayama<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>.

One detail from Weil's page matters more than any timer: "The absolute time you spend on each phase is not important; the ratio of 4:7:8 is important."<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> If holding for seven is a struggle, you speed the whole thing up and keep the shape. The count is a proportion, not a stopwatch reading.

As for effects, a small study in *Physiological Reports* had 43 healthy adults practise 4-7-8 for six cycles per set, three sets; heart rate and systolic blood pressure fell immediately afterwards and high-frequency heart rate variability rose<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> — a short, plausible effect from a short exercise, not a cure for anything.

## Why the eight-count exhale is where people lose the pattern

Every uneven pattern has one phase that breaks first, and in 4-7-8 it is the exhale.

By the time you reach it you have been holding for seven counts. The urge to breathe is building, attention has drifted, and eight counts is a long time to release air slowly. Either you rush — the exhale meant to take eight counts takes five — or the count slips, because your internal clock accelerates under mild air hunger and "eight" arrives well before eight seconds have. Weil's ratio note is why that matters: the exhale is meant to take twice as long as the inhale<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. Compress the eight and you have quietly stopped doing 4-7-8.

Counting in your head also costs attention exactly where you can least spare it: part of your mind runs arithmetic through the phase where the body is asking for air. An external count fixes both problems — something else keeps the number, and you keep the breath.

## Why a screen is the wrong pacer for this one

4-7-8 has a specific setting. People do it in bed, lights off, eyes closed, often lying flat — Weil suggests using it to help you fall asleep<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>, and Cleveland Clinic frames it the same way<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>.

A screen-based breathing app is at odds with all of that. It needs your eyes open, and the first instruction of the practice is to close them. Even at minimum brightness it is a bright object in a dark room, at the moment you are trying to fall asleep. Lying down makes it worse: you either prop the phone somewhere visible or hold your wrist in the air for four rounds.

Audio pacers avoid the light and introduce a different problem: a chime is a public event, and a bedroom often has someone else asleep in it. A tap on the wrist is neither — it does not enter your eyes, your ears, or the room.

Two setups work.

### Setup A — a one-second interval, and count taps

Set the tap interval to **one second** and let each tap be one count.

| Taps | Phase |
|---|---|
| 1–4 | Inhale through the nose |
| 5–11 | Hold |
| 12–19 | Exhale through the mouth, whoosh |
| 20 | Begin the next inhale |

Nineteen taps is one full round. You are still counting, but counting something outside your head that never speeds up when the air runs short — which is what fixes the collapsing exhale.

### Setup B — one 19-second interval, one tap per round

Set the interval to **nineteen seconds** — a full 4-7-8 cycle at one second per count — and treat every tap as "start the next round." Inside the round you keep the inner count yourself.

This is the lighter touch: fewer taps, more silence, room for the breath to find its own shape. It suits people who already know the pattern and want a boundary marker rather than a metronome. If you have slowed your counts past a second each, lengthen the interval to match — a round at 1.5 seconds per count is a 28- or 29-second interval.

## How many rounds, and what minimum to set

Weil is unusually firm about dose. Do the exercise at least twice a day, but **do not do more than four breath cycles at one time for the first month of practice**; after that you can extend to eight<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. Cleveland Clinic's guidance is similar in scale<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. This is a short practice by design.

That translates cleanly into a minimum duration:

| Rounds | Time at 1 s per count | Minimum to set |
|---|---|---|
| 4 (starting dose) | about 76 seconds | a couple of minutes |

## Safety, and what the evidence does and does not say

Weil notes that a little light-headedness when you first breathe this way passes<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>, and Cleveland Clinic says the same about early light-headedness or shortness of breath<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. Stop and breathe normally if it is more than slight, and practise sitting or lying down rather than standing. Never do breath holds in or near water.

On sleep, be careful what is being claimed. NCCIH reports only "a small amount of low-quality evidence that relaxation techniques by themselves can help with chronic insomnia," and names cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia as the most strongly recommended treatment; a persistent sleep problem deserves a clinician rather than a breathing pattern<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. Relaxation techniques are generally considered safe for healthy people, though some report increased anxiety or intrusive thoughts, with rare reports of worsened symptoms in people with epilepsy, certain psychiatric conditions, or a history of trauma<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>.
