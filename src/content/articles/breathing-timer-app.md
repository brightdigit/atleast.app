A good breathing timer app keeps the count so you can keep the breath. Look for custom patterns, phase cues you can follow without staring, and eyes-free operation — the features that matter once you already know how you want to breathe.

Most people arrive at that list after a few sessions with a web animation or a phone chime. The deeper argument for haptic pacing lives in our [breathing pacer](/breathing-pacer) guide. This page is narrower: what to check in a breathwork timer app, why a wrist beat fits established practice, and how a passive haptic timer on Apple Watch maps onto common patterns without pretending to know inhale from hold.

## What a breathing timer app is for

Structured breathwork is timing work. Cleveland Clinic describes box breathing as four equal counts — inhale, hold, exhale, hold — practised for a few rounds once or twice a day<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. The same clinic’s 4-7-8 guidance is inhale for four, hold for seven, exhale for eight<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. A systematic review of slow breathing defines the category as bringing the breath below roughly ten breaths per minute, and links that pace to higher heart rate variability, greater parasympathetic activity, and reductions in arousal and anxiety<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>.

None of that survives a drifted count. Holds run fast under stress. A timer’s job is to hold the structure while you hold the practice — not to teach you a new library of guided content.

That is a different product from Apple’s built-in Breathe session. The Mindfulness app on Apple Watch runs sessions of one to five minutes, lets you change breath rate, and offers haptics set to None, Minimal, or Prominent<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>. It is a fine on-ramp. It is not a custom pattern editor, and it is not built around a floor you choose and silence when you clear it.

## What to look for in a breathing timer app

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Custom timing** | Box, 4-7-8, and round-based work need different counts. A fixed one-size session forces the pattern to fit the app. |
| **Phase cues you can use eyes-closed** | Animations need your eyes. Sound enters the room. A cue on the skin does neither. |
| **Cues during the session** | Knowing where you are mid-round is more useful than one alert at the end. |
| **Ends without an alarm** | An alarm arrives exactly when concentration is deepest. Silence can be the completion signal. |
| **Wrist-native when you can** | Phones ask to be propped, unlocked, and watched. A Watch timer stays on the arm you are already wearing. |
| **Works offline** | Practice happens on retreats, outdoors, and in rooms with no signal. |
| **No account required** | Counting seconds is not a reason to sign in. |
| **Honest about what it cues** | Multi-phase apps that name inhale and exhale are one product. A single-interval pacer is another. Know which you are buying. |

**Phase cues** are the row people confuse most. Some apps play a different sound or show a different animation for inhale versus hold. Others supply one steady beat and leave the labels to you. Both are legitimate — but if your eyes are closed and the room is shared, a single private tap every few seconds often beats a public multi-phase soundtrack.

Apple Watch already treats wrist taps as a first-class channel: you can set haptic intensity to Default, Prominent, or Off, separately from system sounds<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. A breathing timer that uses that channel during the session, not only when it ends, is doing the job eyes-free practice needs.

## Why haptic pacing suits practice you already know

If you already have a pattern, you do not need a coach in your ear. You need a metronome that does not ask for your eyes.

Visual pacers fight the first instruction of almost every technique: close them. Audio pacers fix the eyes and make the session public — or require headphones that turn a quiet practice into something worn. Mental counting is free and unreliable exactly when you started because you were stressed.

A haptic beat sidesteps all three. You feel a tap, you change phase or count, and nothing lights up, chimes, or enters the room. That is why haptic pacing fits *established* practice especially well: the knowledge is already yours; the timer only keeps the rhythm honest.

*AtLeast* is a passive haptic timer for Apple Watch. You set a **minimum duration** (1–60 minutes) and a **tap interval**. Gentle taps mark time on your wrist during the session. When the taps stop, you have reached your minimum. There is no alarm and no sound. Silence is the completion signal.

Against the checklist:

One limit to be clear about: *AtLeast* has **one tap interval per session**. It does not cue inhale, hold, and exhale as separate events. For equal phases that is enough. For uneven patterns you use a practical setup rather than pretending the app follows the shape.

### Practical setups for common patterns

| Pattern | Practical setup on a single-interval timer |
|---|---|
| **Box breathing (4-4-4-4)** | Interval **4 seconds**. Each tap is a phase change — inhale, hold, exhale, hold<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. See the [box breathing timer](/box-breathing-timer) guide. |
| **4-7-8** | Interval **1 second**. Count taps: four in, seven held, eight out<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. Or run one short session per round. |
| **Equal slow breathing** | Interval equal to half a breath cycle (e.g. 5 s in / 5 s out → 5 s taps). Stays inside the slow-breathing range<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>. |
| **Round-based work** | One session per round at a short steady interval, then start the next round when you are ready. |

Set a minimum you will actually sit for — five minutes is a common floor for box work — press Start on the Watch, and let the arm rest where it will be. When the taps stop, you have earned the silence. Stay longer if you want; a minimum is a floor, not a deadline.

## Safety, and what a timer is not

NCCIH notes that relaxation techniques are generally considered safe for healthy people, that most studies report no negative side effects, and that people occasionally report increased anxiety, intrusive thoughts, or fear of losing control — with rare reports that certain techniques may worsen symptoms in people with epilepsy, certain psychiatric conditions, or a history of trauma<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>. Talk to a clinician first if any of that applies.

**Stop if you feel dizzy or light-headed.** Return to normal breathing. And **never practise breath holds in or near water**, including a bath, a pool, or a cold plunge.

*AtLeast* is not a medical device. It does not diagnose, treat, or monitor anything. It marks time on your wrist until you have done at least as much as you promised yourself.
