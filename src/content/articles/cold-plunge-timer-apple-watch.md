Keep the timer on the wrist you are already wearing. In a plunge your hands are wet and losing dexterity, your eyes want to close, and a phone at the rim of the tub is a phone with a future in the water. A watch has none of those problems.

That is why the [cold plunge timer](/cold-plunge-timer) guide argues for wrist-native timing. This page is the Apple Watch specifics: what the built-in Timer does in cold water, where it stops short, and what Apple says about putting the watch under.

## Why the wrist beats the phone at the side of a tub

**The phone has to live somewhere.** At a plunge that is a ledge, a towel, or the lip of a stock tank — every option within splashing distance. A watch is already strapped on.

**Your hands stop cooperating, quickly.** Manual dexterity is impaired once finger temperature drops below 15°C, and in a controlled study of 60-minute head-out immersions, gross dexterity fell 72% and fine dexterity 45% in 10°C water — losses whole-body active heating did not meaningfully prevent<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>. A touchscreen needs a finger both dry and precise; cold water takes both.

**Looking costs you the state you were holding.** The first thirty seconds are spent settling the breath. Opening your eyes and reading small digits undoes it.

A watch answers all three: water resistant enough for the exposure (with caveats below), no touchscreen input once running, and a firm tap on the skin.

## What the built-in Apple Watch Timer does in cold water

Apple's user guide says you can set multiple timers tracking time for up to 24 hours, choosing a quick preset or dialling a custom duration with the Digital Crown<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. In Silent Mode alerts are silenced and wrist taps carry the notification; in Sounds & Haptics you can set wrist tap intensity to Default, Prominent, or Off<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>. Prominent is worth trying before a plunge — cold skin is less sensitive to a light tap.

So: a silent countdown, on your wrist, no phone. That already beats a phone timer at a tub.

Where it stops short is the middle. The built-in Timer produces exactly one signal — a buzz when the countdown expires, meaning *stop*. Before that it tells you nothing, so you either check (raise a wet wrist, read a screen) or spend the session wondering how long is left. It also frames the plunge as something to endure until a bell releases you: the last twenty seconds get spent bracing for the alert instead of breathing. Correct for a boiling egg; the wrong shape for the hard middle of a plunge.

## How AtLeast runs a plunge on the Watch

*AtLeast* is a passive haptic timer for Apple Watch. You set a minimum duration and a tap interval; gentle taps mark time on your wrist during the session, and when the taps stop, you have reached your minimum. No alarm, no sound, no screen to check.

**Taps mean *still going*** — reassurance on a rhythm; a tap every 15 to 30 seconds is the usual choice for a one-to-three-minute session. **Silence means *minimum reached*** — the absence of a signal is the signal. Nothing startles you at the finish, nobody else hears anything, and staying in another thirty seconds becomes a decision rather than a deadline you missed.

Three details make it work at a tub:

- **The face can be off.** Haptics continue through an Extended Runtime Session — nothing lights up in a dim garage.
- **No phone required.** It runs entirely on the Watch and works offline.
- **Optional setup from a dry phone.** Pre-load a timer on the iPhone companion indoors, then press Start on the Watch at the tub. Taps always happen on the wrist.

It requires Apple Watch Series 6 or later on watchOS 26.0 or later, and is a free TestFlight beta today. No accounts; session and haptic logic run on-device; analytics are cookieless.

## Water resistance, honestly

Apple states plainly that "your Apple Watch is water resistant, but not waterproof." Series 2 and later have a 50 metre rating under ISO 22810:2010 and "can be used for shallow-water activities like swimming in a pool or ocean"; Ultra and later are rated to 100 metres; the original Apple Watch and Series 1 carry only an IPX7 rating. Apple also states that water resistance "isn't a permanent condition and can diminish over time," and that Apple Watch "can't be rechecked or resealed for water resistance"<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>.

**Check the rating for your specific model on Apple's support page before you submerge it.** *AtLeast* needs Series 6 or later, which sits inside the 50 metre band, but answer both questions for your own watch.

**Water Lock** locks the screen during a swimming workout and afterwards you "press and hold the Digital Crown to unlock the screen and clear any water," at which point "a series of tones plays to clear any water that remains in the speaker"<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. Whether you enable it for a plunge is your call — we have not verified how it interacts with a running *AtLeast* session.

## Setting up a plunge, step by step

1. **Decide your floor while warm and dry** — a duration you would clear on a middling day, not your aspiration.
2. **Dial the tap interval short** — 15 to 30 seconds for most one-to-three-minute plunges.
3. **Set the session** on the Watch, or on the iPhone companion indoors with dry hands.
4. **Set wrist tap intensity to Prominent** in Settings › Sounds & Haptics if Default is easy to miss<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>.
5. **Press Start on the Watch**, then get in gently rather than jumping<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>.
6. **Close your eyes and breathe.** Let the taps do the timing.
7. **Notice the quiet.** When your wrist has been silent for a few seconds you have cleared your minimum.
8. **Dry the watch afterwards**, following Apple's guidance for your model<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>.

## Safety, which is not optional

*AtLeast* is a timer. Its Terms state it is **not a medical device**, and nothing on this page is medical advice.

- **Never plunge alone.** The British Heart Foundation advises going with a friend or as part of a group<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>.
- **Talk to a clinician first, especially about your heart.** Entering very cold water "can lead to a 'cold shock', which can cause a faster heart rate, higher blood pressure, and shortness of breath"<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>. Heart condition, high blood pressure, or pregnancy: that conversation comes first.
- **Enter gradually and start short.** Sudden immersion triggers the cold shock response — an initial gasp, hypertension, and hyperventilation — and these responses "may act as a precursor to drowning"<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. It habituates after roughly four immersions<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup> — a reason to build up deliberately.
- **Never hyperventilate or breath-hold in water.**
- **Get out if something changes.** Confusion, spreading numbness, or breathing you cannot settle ends the session, whatever the timer says.
