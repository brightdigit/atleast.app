A haptic timer app signals you by touch — taps on your wrist — instead of a chime or a screen. The ones worth installing tap *during* the session, not only when it ends, so you can keep your eyes closed and still know where you are in the time.

For the wider view of vibrating timers, including physical pager-style timers and phones, start with our guide to [a timer that vibrates](/timer-that-vibrates). This page is narrower: what a haptic timer app is, what separates a good one from a muted one, and how to get one running today.

## What a haptic timer app is — and who it's for

Most timers assume you are available to be interrupted: they make a noise, or they light up a screen. A haptic timer app assumes the opposite — eyes closed, hands busy, room shared — and delivers the whole signal through skin. Apple's own Taptic Time conveys the hour and minute through long and short taps so someone blind or visually impaired can tell the time "without anyone else hearing it"<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>. A haptic timer app is that idea applied to a session rather than a clock.

People who look for one tend to already have a practice:

- **Meditators** who do not want a bell at the moment attention settled.
- **Breathwork practitioners** who need a steady beat without watching an animation.
- **Cold plunge and sauna users** with wet hands and no interest in touching a phone.
- **Lifters, yogis, and focus-block workers** who need equal time without broadcasting an alarm.
- **Anyone who is deaf or hard of hearing**, for whom a sound-based timer is simply absent.

In an anaesthesiology simulation, Ferris and Sarter found tactile displays improved multitasking over a visual-and-auditory-only baseline — and that a *continuously informing* tactile display supported better performance than a tactile alarm<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. Continuous information versus a single alarm is the same split you will find between haptic timer apps.

## What to look for in a haptic timer app

| What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Haptic cues *during* the session** | The single most useful feature, and the rarest. Without it you get one buzz at the end. |
| **Silent by design, not by settings** | An app that is haptic-only cannot forget. One that vibrates *and* rings only goes quiet when you silence the whole device. |
| **Works with the screen off** | If cues stop when the display sleeps, you have to keep your wrist up. |
| **Standalone on the Watch** | A companion that needs a phone in the room is a phone in the room. |
| **Works offline** | Practice happens in basements, on retreats, and outdoors. |
| **No account** | Nothing about counting to ten requires a sign-in. |
| **Clear price** | Free with a subscription, one-off purchase, and free beta are all fine — know which. |

**Silent by design** is not the same as silenced: on Apple Watch, Silent Mode silences alerts while you still receive haptic notifications<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>, but it is a switch. An app built haptic-first has nothing to switch. Wrist tap intensity can be set to Default, Prominent, or Off<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>; Prominent is worth trying if you wear the band loose or are about to be cold.
