A timer that vibrates signals you through touch instead of sound. Your phone can do it, physical pager-style timers do it, and the Apple Watch does it through Silent Mode. They differ in one thing that matters: whether the vibration arrives *during* your session, or only to end it.

## Why people want a timer that taps instead of rings

The reason is almost always the same, whatever the practice. You are doing something that requires your attention to stay where it is, and a sound would take it away — either from you, or from everyone else in the room.

Meditators sit with their eyes closed and want the sit to end without a bell startling them out of it. Breathwork practitioners need to know a round is running without opening their eyes to check. Someone in a cold plunge has wet hands and no interest in reaching for anything. A teacher timing a group activity does not want a chime cutting across a quiet classroom. Someone in a shared office wants a focus block that ends without announcing itself to three desks over. And for people who are deaf or hard of hearing, a sound-based timer is not quiet — it is simply absent.

The research on interruption backs up the instinct. In a controlled study using EEG, task interruptions measurably reduced performance on resumption, and repeated interruptions compounded the effect<sup><a href="#ref-10">[10]</a></sup>. In hospitals, the same dynamic has a name: alarm fatigue, where frequent and non-actionable alarms lead to desensitisation, and where poor alarm audibility and design are documented contributors<sup><a href="#ref-9">[9]</a></sup>. A timer alarm is a small version of the same problem. It is loud, it is unmissable, and it arrives at exactly the moment you were most absorbed.

Touch offers a way out. Vibrotactile signalling has been studied as an alternative channel precisely because it does not compete for the eyes and ears. In an anaesthesiology simulation, tactile displays improved multitasking performance over visual-and-auditory-only configurations — and continuously informing tactile displays, which kept signalling state rather than only alarming, outperformed a tactile alarm on objective measures<sup><a href="#ref-8">[8]</a></sup>. That distinction — continuous information versus a single alarm — turns out to be the whole story with timers, too.

## The options, honestly compared

There are four practical ways to get a timer that vibrates. Each is genuinely good at something.

### Your phone on vibrate

Every phone has a timer, and every phone can vibrate. It costs nothing and you already have it. It is the right answer for the kitchen, the laundry, or anything where the phone will sit on a hard surface within earshot — a phone buzzing against a countertop is loud, which is often the point.

It is a poor answer for practice. The phone has to be near you and touching something to be felt, which means it is also near you and available to be picked up. You need the screen to set it, and usually the screen again to dismiss it. And a phone on a yoga mat, beside a plunge tub, or on a desk during a focus block is the exact object you were trying to get away from.

### Physical vibrating timers

Dedicated vibrating timers are a real category and they solve real problems. Clip-on kitchen and classroom timers with a pager-style motor let a teacher run timed rotations without a chime, and let a cook work in a noisy kitchen. They are cheap, single-purpose, need no phone, and never update themselves.

They matter most in accessibility. The NIDCD notes that alerting devices "use sound, light, vibrations, or a combination of these techniques to let someone know when a particular event is occurring," and that clocks and wake-up alarm systems let a person choose to wake to flashing lights, horns, or a gentle shaking<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>. For someone who is deaf or hard of hearing, a vibrating alerting device is not a preference — it is the functional equivalent of everyone else's alarm.

Their limits are physical. You have to be in contact with the device, which rules out anything wet, anything where you move, and anything where you would rather not clip a plastic box to yourself. And almost all of them signal one thing: the end.

### The Apple Watch built-in Timer

The Watch already has a timer, and it can already be silent. You set one with Siri or in the Timers app, choosing a quick duration or dialling a custom one with the Digital Crown; multiple timers can run for up to 24 hours<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. Turn on Silent Mode — from Control Center, or in Sounds & Haptics — and alerts are silenced while you still receive haptic notifications<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>. You can set the intensity of wrist taps to Default, Prominent, or Off<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. Covering the display with your palm for three seconds mutes a notification, and on newer models a wrist flick will stop a timer outright<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>.

That is a genuinely good silent timer, and for most people it is enough. Note two things about what it is, though. First, it is a countdown to an end-of-time alert: the buzz arrives once, when the time expires, and its meaning is *stop*. Nothing happens in between. Second, if you want to know how much time is left, you look — the Always On display keeps the face visible with the wrist down, but it dims, and reading it means raising your wrist or tapping the screen<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>.

For a boiling egg, that is exactly right. For a twenty-minute sit, it means the only signal the timer ever gives you is the one that ends the session.

### Dedicated haptic timer apps

The fourth option is an app built around the haptics rather than around the countdown. Instead of one buzz at the end, it taps at an interval you choose for the length of the session. The taps are not instructions. They carry no information beyond "time is still passing," which is what makes them ignorable in the good sense — you feel them, you register them, and you carry on.

*AtLeast* is one of these. You set a minimum duration and a tap interval; gentle taps mark time on your wrist while you practise; when the taps stop, you have reached your minimum. There is no alarm and no sound at any point.

## The settings that actually matter

Whatever you choose, these are the four questions worth asking before you trust a timer with a session.

**Is it vibrate-only, or vibrate-and-also-sound?** Many timers vibrate *in addition to* ringing, and only go quiet when you remember to silence the whole device. A timer that is haptic by design cannot forget. On Apple Watch, Silent Mode is the switch that makes the built-in timer haptic-only<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>; on a phone, check that vibrate does not simply mean "vibrate first, then ring."

**Does it need the screen?** Setting a timer with your eyes open is fine. Needing the screen mid-session is not. Ask whether the timer keeps running, and keeps signalling, once the display has gone dark and you have put your hands back where they belong.

**Does it wake the screen to alert you?** In a dark room, at a retreat, or beside someone sleeping, a screen lighting up is an interruption in exactly the same way a sound is — just to a different sense and a different audience.

**Can you use it without looking at all?** This is the real test. Apple's own Taptic Time feature exists for precisely this reason: it "gives people who are blind or visually impaired another way to tell time without anyone else hearing it," conveying the hour and minute through long and short taps while the Watch is in Silent Mode<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. Time can be delivered entirely through touch. A timer built on that premise never asks you to open your eyes.

## Haptic nudges during a session versus an end-of-time buzz

This is the distinction that separates the two kinds of vibrating timer, and it is worth being precise about.

An **end-of-time buzz** is a single event. It arrives once, it arrives at the end, and it means stop. Because it is the only signal, it has to be strong enough not to be missed — which is why it lands hardest at the moment your attention is deepest. It is also silent about everything before it: for the whole session you have no idea how far along you are, so you either check, or you wonder. Checking breaks the state. Wondering also breaks the state.

A **haptic nudge** is a rhythm. A tap every thirty seconds, or every minute, or every four seconds if you are pacing breaths. Each one says the same thing, so there is nothing to decode and no reason to react. What it removes is the wondering. You stop asking how long it has been, because your wrist keeps answering.

*AtLeast* inverts the usual ending as well. You set a **minimum** rather than a deadline — a floor, not a ceiling. The taps run for the length of that minimum and then simply stop. **The silence is the completion signal.** Nothing arrives to interrupt you; something you had grown used to is absent, and that absence tells you that you have reached what you committed to. If you want to keep going, nothing has told you to stop.

That framing suits the way the evidence describes practice, too. Brief and consistent is what has been studied: a randomised trial of 13 minutes of daily meditation in non-experienced meditators found improvements in attention, working memory, and mood after eight weeks<sup><a href="#ref-11">[11]</a></sup>, and reviews of mindfulness programs report they can be better than no treatment and comparable to established evidence-based therapies for anxiety and depression, with mixed results for pain<sup><a href="#ref-12">[12]</a></sup>. A minimum you can actually keep is worth more than a target you negotiate with. ## How to set up AtLeast

Three decisions, none of which you have to revisit mid-session.

1. **Pick your minimum.** Anywhere from 1 to 60 minutes. Choose the number you will actually sit, plunge, or hold for on an ordinary day — not the one you would like to be the kind of person who does.
2. **Pick your tap interval.** Longer intervals (a minute or more) suit sitting and focus blocks, where the taps are just a background pulse. Shorter intervals suit breathwork and cold exposure, where you are counting against them. One tap interval runs for the whole session.
3. **Start it on the Watch and put your hands down.** The taps continue with the watch face off, for the whole minimum. Then they stop.

It runs entirely on the Apple Watch and works offline, with no phone nearby. There is an optional iPhone companion if you would rather pick or pre-load a timer on the larger screen and mirror the live session — but the taps always happen on the wrist. It requires Apple Watch Series 6 or later running watchOS 26.0 or later, and it is a free TestFlight beta today.
