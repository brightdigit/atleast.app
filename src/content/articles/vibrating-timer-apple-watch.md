Yes — an Apple Watch timer vibrates without sound once you turn on Silent Mode, because haptic notifications still arrive. The limit is that the built-in Timer gives you one buzz at the end. Nothing marks time while the session is actually running.

That difference matters most on the wrist, which is why people search for a vibrating timer for Apple Watch rather than simply muting a phone. For the wider comparison — phone, physical pager timers, and Watch side by side — start with our guide to [a timer that vibrates](/timer-that-vibrates). This page is about the Watch: what it can do that a pocket cannot, and where its own timer stops short.

## What a vibrating timer on the wrist does that a phone in a pocket can't

A phone on vibrate is really a small speaker. It buzzes against whatever surface it is resting on, and that surface does most of the work. Put the same phone on a folded towel or a yoga mat and the alert can fade to nothing; put it in a jacket on a hook and it is gone.

A watch is different in one structural way: it is strapped to you. The signal has nowhere to go except into your skin. Three consequences follow, and they are the whole case for timing from the wrist.

**It reaches you with your eyes closed.** Meditation, breathwork, and mobility holds all involve deliberately giving up your vision. A vibrating phone still asks you to find it, wake it, and read it; a tap on the wrist needs no interpretation. Apple builds on this premise elsewhere in watchOS: Taptic Time exists to give "people who are blind or visually impaired another way to tell time without anyone else hearing it," conveying hours and minutes through long and short taps while the Watch is in Silent Mode<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. Time can be delivered entirely through touch.

**It reaches you with your hands busy or wet.** A cold plunge, a kettlebell, a plank, a pair of gloves — in each your hands are unavailable, and in some a phone should not be near you at all. The wrist keeps working. Nothing to unlock, nothing to dry off first.

**It reaches only you.** In a shared office, a studio, a bedroom with someone asleep, an alarm is a broadcast. A wrist tap is private by construction — nobody else in the room knows your session ended. A screen lighting up is no substitute in a dark room; it announces itself to a different sense but the same audience.

There is research behind the intuition that touch suits this kind of low-stakes, continuous information. In an anaesthesiology simulation, Ferris and Sarter found tactile displays supported multitasking better than visual-and-auditory-only configurations, and that *continuously informing* tactile displays — ones that kept signalling state rather than only firing an alarm — outperformed a tactile alarm on objective measures<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>. Continuous information versus a single alarm is exactly the split between the two kinds of vibrating timer you can run on a Watch.

## How to make the built-in Apple Watch Timer vibrate without sound

You do not need an app for a silent countdown. The Watch already does this, and it takes about thirty seconds to set up.

1. **Turn on Silent Mode.** Press the side button to open Control Center, then tap the Silent Mode button<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup>. Alerts are silenced and you still receive haptic notifications<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> — which is the whole trick. You can also find the same switch in Settings › Sounds & Haptics<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>.
2. **Set the tap strength.** In Sounds & Haptics, wrist tap intensity can be set to Default, Prominent, or Off<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>. Prominent is worth trying if you wear the band loose, or if you are about to be cold and less sensitive to a light tap.
3. **Start the timer.** Open the Timers app and pick a quick duration, or tap hours, minutes, or seconds and turn the Digital Crown to dial a custom one. You can run multiple timers, tracking up to 24 hours<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>.

Two details while it runs. With Always On the time stays visible with your wrist down, but the display dims and Timer complications round what they show to the minute<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup> — a glance gives you less precision than you might assume. To silence an alert, rest your palm on the display for three seconds; on supported models a quick turn of the wrist and back stops a timer outright<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>.

**The limit.** All of that produces exactly one signal: a buzz when the countdown expires, meaning *stop*. Between pressing start and that moment the timer tells you nothing, so you either check — breaking the state you were protecting — or spend the session wondering how long is left, which breaks it just as effectively. For a boiling egg that is correct behaviour. For a twenty-minute sit, the only thing the timer ever says arrives at the moment your attention is deepest.

## How AtLeast's haptic nudges work mid-practice

*AtLeast* is built around the other half of that split. Instead of a countdown to an alert, you set a **minimum** duration — anywhere from 1 to 60 minutes — and a **tap interval**. Gentle taps mark time on your wrist while you practise. When the taps stop, you have reached your minimum.

The taps carry no instruction. Each says the same thing — time is still passing — so there is nothing to decode and no reason to react, which makes them easy to sit with rather than something to brace against. What they remove is the wondering: you stop asking how far along you are, because your wrist keeps answering.

The ending is inverted too. Nothing arrives to interrupt you; something you had grown used to is simply absent, and that absence is the completion signal. If you want to keep going, nothing has told you to stop — a floor, not a ceiling.

Practically, on the Watch:

- The haptics continue with the **watch face off**, using an Extended Runtime Session, so you never need to raise your wrist.
- It runs **entirely on the Apple Watch** and works **offline**, with no phone nearby.
- There is an **optional iPhone companion** if you would rather pick or pre-load a timer on the larger screen and mirror the live session — but the taps always happen on the wrist.
- It requires **Apple Watch Series 6 or later running watchOS 26.0 or later**, and it is a free TestFlight beta today.

## Picking a tap interval by practice

One tap interval runs for the whole session, so choose it for the job the taps are doing. Long intervals fade into the background; short intervals are something you can count against. These are starting points from how people practise, not documented limits — the app accepts whatever you set.

| Practice | Suggested minimum | Suggested interval | What the taps are for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seated meditation | 10–20 min | 60 s | A quiet pulse in the background; you stop tracking elapsed time |
| Breathwork pacing | 5–10 min | 4–10 s | Something steady to count breaths against |
| Cold plunge | 2–5 min | 15–30 s | Reassurance that time is moving when it feels like it isn't |
| Focus work | 25–45 min | 5 min | Sparse checkpoints that don't break the block |

Two rules of thumb. If you notice each tap as an event, the interval is too short. If you find yourself wondering how long it has been, it is too long. In cold water, a shorter interval and Prominent tap strength<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup> are worth it — cold skin is less sensitive than warm.

## Privacy

*AtLeast* has no accounts and nothing to sign in to. The session and haptic logic run on-device, and the timer works offline with no phone nearby. Analytics are cookieless Plausible — website pageviews and outbound-link clicks, plus app events from the iPhone and Apple Watch apps via AviaryInsights, including session configuration such as duration and tap interval. None of it is used for advertising or cross-app tracking. A timer on your wrist should not need to know who you are to count to ten.

If the built-in Watch timer in Silent Mode does what you need, use it — it is free and already on your wrist. *AtLeast* is for the narrower case where the end of the session is the part you are protecting.
