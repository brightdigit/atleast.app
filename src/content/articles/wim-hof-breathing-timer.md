## What a Wim Hof round actually is

A Wim Hof round has three parts: about thirty deep breaths in a row, a breath hold after the last exhale until you feel the urge to breathe, then one recovery breath held for about fifteen seconds<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. The official guidance suggests three to four rounds in a sitting, practised seated or lying down with an empty stomach when you can<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. Medical News Today describes the same shape for beginners — thirty to forty breaths, an open-ended retention, a fifteen-second recovery hold — repeated three or four times<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>.

Only the first part of that is paced. The retention is deliberately open-ended — you hold until the urge arrives, not until a clock says so — so a [breathing pacer](/breathing-pacer) has a narrow job: carry the thirty breaths, leave you alone through the hold, and give you a quiet nudge when it is time to start the next round.

That is different from [4-7-8](/4-7-8-breathing-timer), where every count is fixed. Here the hard part is not arithmetic inside a single breath; it is staying in the rhythm of the round without looking down mid-hold to check whether the session is still running.

## Why a screen mid-round breaks the flow

Wim Hof practice asks for closed eyes and a clear mind during the breathing phase<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. A phone animation fights that: you open your eyes to confirm the count, the brightness arrives, and attention splits between the breath and the UI. Between rounds the cost is worse. The retention is where people most want to stay inward — and where a glowing screen is the last thing that belongs in the room.

Audio cues avoid the light and introduce a different problem. A chime at the end of a hold is a public event, and a guided track keeps talking through the silence you came for. Counting thirty breaths in your head works until it does not: under mild air hunger the internal clock speeds up, and "thirty" arrives early.

What you want mid-session is something you can feel without looking — a tap that means "one breath" or "start the next round," then silence while you hold.

## What *AtLeast* can and cannot do for this pattern

*AtLeast* is a passive haptic timer for Apple Watch. You set a minimum duration, from one to sixty minutes, and a tap interval. Gentle taps mark time on your wrist while the session runs; when they stop, you have reached your minimum. No alarm, no sound, no screen to check.

Be clear about the limit. *AtLeast* has **one tap interval per session**. It does not know Wim Hof phases, and it will not cue "inhale," "hold," or "recovery" as distinct signals. There is no multi-phase pattern engine. What you get is a reliable external beat you count against — or a quiet boundary between rounds. Two setups work.

### Setup A — a two-second interval during the breathing phase

Set the tap interval to **two seconds** and treat each tap as one breath. Thirty taps is one breathing phase at a steady pace<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. When the thirtieth tap lands, exhale and begin the retention. **Ignore the taps during the hold** — the retention is meant to end when you feel the urge, not when the watch says so<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. After the recovery breath, the next run of taps carries the next round.

Set the minimum long enough to cover all your rounds plus the retentions. Ten to fifteen minutes is typical for three or four rounds; because it is a floor rather than a deadline, silence arrives when you have earned it, and nothing forces you to stop mid-round.

### Setup B — one short session per round

Start a fresh session at the top of each round. Choose a minimum of a couple of minutes and a two-second interval so the taps run only while you are actively breathing. When you finish the recovery breath, stop — or let the taps go quiet — and start again for round two.

This is the lighter touch: fewer continuous taps through the holds, a clean break between rounds, and a rhythm that matches how the practice is already structured. It suits people who already know the thirty-breath count and mainly want a nudge that says "begin" without a phone face in view.

Neither setup pretends the app is following the method. You still own the phases; the wrist owns the count.

## Setting the floor, and keeping your eyes closed

Three product details matter for this practice more than for a desk timer.

The taps continue with the watch face off, so your arm can stay at your side through the hold. The session runs entirely on the Watch and works offline — no phone needs to be in the room. The optional iPhone companion is only for pre-loading a timer when dry hands are handy; the taps always happen on the wrist.

Haptic strength is a watchOS setting: Apple Watch lets you set wrist-tap intensity to Default, Prominent, or Off, and Silent Mode silences alerts without removing the taps you feel<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>. There is nothing to sign in to — no account, no streak, no feed. Session and haptic logic run on-device; analytics are cookieless and never used for advertising or cross-app tracking. *AtLeast* requires an Apple Watch Series 6 or later on watchOS 26.0 or later, and it is a free TestFlight beta today.

## Safety first — and what the evidence does not claim

This section is not optional.

The Wim Hof Method's own page warns that intense rounds can in rare cases lead to loss of consciousness, and that this is dangerous in or near water or while driving; it instructs you to always practise seated or lying down in a neutral space such as a living room<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. Medical News Today is equally direct: the method is unsafe in water or in any setting where losing consciousness could cause harm, including a cold shower when you are alone and could fall<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>.

**Never do Wim Hof breathing or breath holds in water.** Not in a bath, a pool, an ice bath, or a cold plunge. Do not combine the breathing protocol with immersion. Practise dry, seated or lying down, then take cold exposure separately if that is part of your practice.

Light-headedness and tingling in the fingers or feet can occur and usually pass when you return to normal breathing<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup>. Stop if symptoms are more than slight. Talk to a clinician before starting if you are pregnant, have a history of fainting, blood-pressure issues, or heart disease<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup>.

On outcomes, keep claims modest. A small controlled study found that volunteers trained in related breathing and cold-exposure techniques could voluntarily raise epinephrine and attenuate parts of an experimentally induced inflammatory response<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup> — interesting physiology in a lab setting, not a prescription. NCCIH notes that relaxation techniques are generally considered safe for healthy people, while also recording occasional reports of increased anxiety or intrusive thoughts, and cautioning that some techniques may worsen symptoms for people with epilepsy, certain psychiatric conditions, or a history of trauma<sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>.

*AtLeast* is a timer. It is not a medical device, and it does not diagnose, treat, or monitor anything. It counts, quietly, on your wrist — which for Wim Hof rounds is the one job worth outsourcing between the breaths and the silence.
